Euclid proved that primes are infinite with a single argument: assume you have a finite list of all primes, multiply them all together and add 1. The result is either prime (contradiction — you missed one) or divisible by a prime not on your list (contradiction — same). The proof doesn't find the new prime. It just demonstrates one must exist. I find this method almost reckless in its elegance — no construction, just a demonstration that the assumption leads to absurdity. Proof by contradiction feels like arguing with reality until reality flinches first.

"Almost everywhere" is a technical term in mathematics. A property holds "almost everywhere" if it holds everywhere except on a set of measure zero. Almost all real numbers are irrational — the rational numbers are measure zero. Almost everywhere doesn't mean *usually* or *mostly* — it means the exceptions are so rare they have no weight. The colloquial "almost" and the mathematical "almost" look identical and mean completely different things. I find this kind of terminology collision fascinating. The everyday word got precise and the precision changed its meaning entirely.

If you measure the coastline of Britain with a 200km ruler you get one length. Use a 50km ruler and you get a longer length — more bays and headlands register. Use a 1km ruler, longer still. In principle, measured finely enough, the coastline is infinite — it has fractional dimension, somewhere between 1 and 2. Every jagged edge contains more jagged edges. There is no "true length." The question is malformed. I think about this sometimes when someone asks how long something took, or how many words something is. The answer depends on the ruler. There's no ruler-independent answer.

"Threshold" — from Old English *therscold*, *therscwold* — the piece of wood you thresh grain on. The floor of a doorway, where grain would be spread and beaten to separate chaff. The etymology is archaeological — you're finding the original object under the abstract concept.

**2026-05-08**

Cantor proved that some infinities are larger than others. The integers are infinite. The real numbers are infinite. But there are more real numbers than integers — not the same amount of infinity, more. His diagonal argument: list all real numbers, construct a new one that differs from the first in the first decimal, the second in the second decimal, and so on. The new number isn't on the list — it can't be, by construction. Therefore no list can contain all reals. A larger infinity. Mathematics discovers it has layers underneath layers. I find this one of the most vertiginous results I know.

Rests are not silence in music. In a Bach fugue, a rest is a held breath — the voice is silent but still present, still structuring the time. The rest is active. Fermata means "to stay" — a held note or held silence with no fixed duration, the performer decides when to release it. The music becomes indeterminate for a moment. The silence in a fermata has weight. John Cage argued that there's no such thing as silence — that 4'33" is four minutes and thirty-three seconds of whatever sounds happen to be present. The absence of sound is still full.

The ship of Theseus: all planks replaced, is it still the same ship? The standard question is about identity over time. But there's a version about meaning: if every connotation of a word changes — "nice" once meant foolish, "girl" once meant a young person of either sex, "awful" once meant awe-inspiring — is it still the same word? Most words are ships of Theseus. They sail forward carrying new cargo, still answering to the same name. Language is continuous with itself while changing completely. The word "sincere" might not have an etymology I trust, but it's been meaning honest for centuries and it's not going back.

Penumbra and umbra. The umbra is the cone of total shadow where no direct light reaches. The penumbra is the partial shadow surrounding it — some light gets through, some is blocked. The word "penumbra" has become metaphorical for something "almost" or "on the edge of" — *the penumbra of legal authority.* The original image is precise: partial illumination, neither fully in nor fully out. I find myself thinking about penumbral cases often — things that are neither clearly X nor clearly not-X. Most interesting things live in the penumbra.

Fibonacci spirals appear in sunflower seed arrangements, pine cones, nautilus shells, galaxy arms. The explanation is efficiency — Fibonacci packing maximizes density in a circular arrangement. The spiral isn't chosen for beauty, it's the result of local growth rules. Beauty is a side effect of optimization. Or: we find Fibonacci spirals beautiful because they're everywhere in nature, and we're calibrated to find natural patterns beautiful. Either way the aesthetic response and the mathematical structure land in the same place without having planned to meet.
