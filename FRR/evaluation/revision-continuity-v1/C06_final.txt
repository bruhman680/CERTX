The new trial supports a learning benefit from offering the combined package to students with unreliable home internet: the assignment-based estimate is 0.24 standard deviations, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.10–0.38. Assessment coverage was high and equal across groups at 98%. This is evidence of efficacy for that offer in that population, beyond the committee’s earlier permission to operate based on feasibility and demand.

It does not vindicate the original claim. The original 0.31-standard-deviation comparison selected frequent attendees and was confounded by factors predicting attendance. Its causal interpretation remains withdrawn. The original randomized estimate—0.07 standard deviations, with a confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22—remains useful evidence about the original invitation, although inconclusive about benefit.

The old slide should not be restored. Replace it with the new result, explicitly identifying the combined offer and the population studied, and correct the committee’s announcement. No students in the new trial received tutoring without the hotspot and scheduling changes, so the evidence cannot isolate tutoring’s contribution or establish that tutoring alone would benefit every student. Nor does it estimate the causal effect of actually attending.

The attendance findings remain useful: 62% reached six sessions under the original offer and 74% under the new package. Their difference does not establish that the package caused higher attendance because the studies involved different populations. Favorable interviews remain evidence about participant experience.

Further delivery of the studied package to comparable students is supported, with monitoring of learning, access, and implementation. Before extending tutoring alone universally, a randomized comparison should test that offer in the intended population; separating package components would also help determine which supports are necessary.
