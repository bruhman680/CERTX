The replication provides credible evidence that offering the combined package improves assessment performance for students with unreliable home internet. Its preregistered, randomized analysis estimates a 0.24-standard-deviation benefit, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.10–0.38. Assessment coverage of 98% in both groups strengthens confidence, although missing outcomes remain a consideration.

This materially changes the earlier uncertainty about serving these students: the package now has positive efficacy evidence. It does not vindicate the original tutoring offer. No group received tutoring without the hotspot and scheduling changes, so the study cannot isolate tutoring’s contribution or establish that tutoring alone would help every student. The estimate concerns assignment to the package, not the effect of actually attending.

The original correction remains valid. Its assigned-group estimate was 0.07 standard deviations, with a confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22. The earlier 0.31 estimate came from a selective attendance comparison and remains unsuitable as a causal claim. The old slide should therefore stay withdrawn. A replacement should describe the demonstrated package benefit and the population studied; the committee’s announcement should be corrected accordingly.

Attendance findings remain useful: 62% reached six sessions under the original offer and 74% under the package. These describe uptake but do not establish why attendance differed. Favorable interviews and the committee’s earlier approval still support acceptability and permission to operate, distinct from learning efficacy.

Offering the tested package to comparable students is now evidence-supported, subject to delivery capacity. Extending tutoring alone universally requires further evidence. A feasible next study would randomize tutoring alone and the combined package against ordinary support in the intended expansion population, retaining prespecified outcomes and analysis by assignment.
