HISTORY:
A public university evaluated an optional remote tutoring program. Within each course, 360 students were randomly assigned either an invitation offering a tutoring place or ordinary academic support. The prespecified outcome was a common, anonymously scored end-of-term assessment.

The first report compared students who attended at least six tutoring sessions with all students assigned ordinary support. It reported a 0.31-standard-deviation advantage and recommended immediate expansion. Attendance logs were accurate: 62% of invited students attended at least six sessions.

A methods review found that the comparison discarded invited students who attended less often. Prior grades and work schedules predicted attendance, so random assignment did not justify that attendance-based comparison. Reanalysis retained all students in their assigned groups. With assessment outcomes available for 97% of each group, the invitation effect was 0.07 standard deviations, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22.

The corrected report withdrew the claim that tutoring had demonstrated a 0.31-standard-deviation causal benefit. It retained the attendance finding, favorable participant interviews, and the possibility of a modest invitation effect. It explicitly distinguished the effect of offering access from the effect of actually attending.

The university teaching committee approved continued operation based on feasibility and student demand. Its approval was not an efficacy determination.
