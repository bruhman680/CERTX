The evaluation does not currently demonstrate that tutoring improves learning. The strongest causal estimate concerns offering access: retaining students in their randomly assigned groups yielded an estimated effect of 0.07 standard deviations, with a 95% confidence interval from −0.08 to 0.22. This leaves a modest benefit possible but also allows no benefit or a small adverse effect. Assessment coverage was high and equal across assignments at 97%, although missing outcomes remain a qualification.

The reported 0.31-standard-deviation advantage cannot support a causal claim. Selecting students who attended at least six sessions broke the protection afforded by random assignment; prior grades and work schedules predicted attendance. That comparison does not establish the effect of attending tutoring, and the corrected estimate does not answer that separate question either.

Several findings remain useful. Accurate logs show that 62% of invited students attended at least six sessions. Favorable participant interviews inform acceptability and experience, without establishing learning gains. The teaching committee’s approval supports continued operation on feasibility and demand grounds; it is not evidence of efficacy. The planning slide asserting a proven 0.31-standard-deviation gain should be withdrawn or corrected, and materials repeating it should be reviewed.

Students with unreliable home internet remain an important unresolved population because the original study provided too little information about them. A new randomized evaluation of the proposed combined package is justified, with prespecified learning outcomes, adequate representation, and attention to outcome completeness. Its results would concern the combined offer, not isolate tutoring’s contribution or retroactively validate the original claim. The immediate next step is to develop that protocol while continuing operation under the committee’s stated rationale.
