The pilot supports a decline in recorded operational alerts, from 30/120 to 18/120, and an independently confirmed reduction in alert-related dispatch calls, from 43 to 27. These are useful operational findings, but they do not establish that insulation reduced the prespecified temperature-exposure endpoint.

The original claim of 40% fewer excursions requires withdrawal because firmware changed the alert threshold from two consecutive minutes above 8°C to three. The safety officer authorized that change for dispatch operations; this neither amended the research endpoint nor established that shorter excursions were harmless. The unchanged dashboard claim, “Validated insulation benefit: 40% fewer excursions,” is therefore unsupported and should be corrected.

Applying the original two-minute endpoint to recoverable records yields 16/80 shipments versus 15/80. Those similar observed proportions do not establish a substantial reduction, but neither do they prove no benefit. Records are missing disproportionately from older devices, so the recovered subsets may not represent all shipments. The sequential comparison also limits causal attribution.

A potentially important benefit on hot routes remains unresolved because hot-weather shipments were uncommon and no suitable comparison was available. A feasible next step is to complete the proposed study using independent loggers and concurrent randomized comparisons, retaining the original endpoint and ensuring adequate hot-route coverage. Meanwhile, assess whether additional records can be recovered and examine how plausible outcomes among missing shipments would change the pilot’s interpretation. Operational alerts and dispatch calls should continue to be reported separately from the research endpoint.
