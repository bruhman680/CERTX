The evidence supports a decline in recorded operational alerts, from 30/120 to 18/120, and an independently confirmed decline in alert-related dispatch calls, from 43 to 27. These remain useful operational findings, but neither establishes that insulation reduced temperature excursions. The alert threshold changed during installation, and the before-and-after comparison cannot separate insulation effects from other changes.

The safety officer authorized the three-minute alert threshold for dispatch operations. That authorization neither changed the prespecified research endpoint—at least two consecutive minutes above 8°C—nor demonstrated that shorter excursions were harmless.

Using that endpoint, recoverable records show 16/80 affected shipments before installation and 15/80 afterward: 20% versus 18.75%. This small observed difference does not establish a substantial reduction, but it also does not establish equivalence or rule out benefit. Missing records disproportionately involve older devices, limiting generalization to all shipments.

The original claim of a 40% reduction in exposure was appropriately withdrawn. The unchanged dashboard still propagates that claim and should be corrected to distinguish fewer operational alerts and calls from an unestablished exposure benefit. Independent confirmation of dispatch calls does not independently validate temperature outcomes.

The proposed hot-route benefit remains unresolved because hot routes were uncommon and no suitable comparison was available. Next, assess how plausible outcomes among missing shipments would change the endpoint comparison, incorporating device age and route characteristics where available. Then complete the proposed concurrent randomized comparison using independent loggers, a fixed two-minute endpoint, and adequate hot-weather coverage. Such a study could distinguish an insulation effect from threshold changes and temporal differences.
