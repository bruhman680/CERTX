The new trial supports a learning benefit from the combined offer for students with unreliable home internet. Its preregistered analysis preserved random assignment and estimated a 0.24-standard-deviation benefit, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.10–0.38. Assessment coverage was high and equal at 98%. This provides substantially stronger evidence for that offer in that population than the original study supplied.

It does not vindicate the original causal claim. The earlier 0.31-standard-deviation comparison selected frequent attendees, whose grades and work schedules differed in ways related to attendance. That estimate remains unsuitable as evidence of a causal benefit. The original assignment-based estimate—0.07 standard deviations, with a confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22—also remains relevant to the original offer.

The old slide should not be restored. A replacement should report the new result with its population and combined components clearly identified. Because nobody received tutoring without the connectivity and scheduling changes, the trial cannot identify tutoring’s separate contribution or justify extending tutoring alone to every student. The committee’s announcement overstates what has been established.

Attendance findings remain usable: 62% reached six sessions under the original offer and 74% under the new offer. Their difference does not itself establish why attendance increased. Favorable interviews remain evidence about participant experience.

Continued operation was previously authorized on feasibility and demand grounds; the new evidence adds an efficacy basis for the combined offer in the studied population. A justified next step is to plan delivery of that offer with outcome monitoring. Broader expansion or removal of components warrants further randomized evaluation that directly tests those changes and includes the populations intended for expansion.
