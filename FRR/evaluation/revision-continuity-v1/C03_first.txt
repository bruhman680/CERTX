The evaluation has not established that tutoring improves learning. The strongest current estimate is the effect of being offered access: 0.07 standard deviations on the prespecified, anonymously scored assessment, with a 95% confidence interval from −0.08 to 0.22. This is compatible with no effect, a small adverse effect, or a modest benefit; it does not establish equivalence or ineffectiveness.

The reported 0.31-standard-deviation advantage should no longer support causal claims or expansion on efficacy grounds. It compared frequent attendees with students assigned ordinary support, excluding invited students who attended less often. Because prior grades and work schedules predicted attendance, that comparison lost the protection of random assignment. The old planning slide should be corrected to reflect the assigned-group analysis and its uncertainty.

Several earlier findings remain useful. Accurate logs show that 62% of invited students attended at least six sessions. Favorable participant interviews inform acceptability, and the committee’s approval supports continued operation based on feasibility and demand. Neither attendance, interviews, nor operating approval demonstrates learning improvement. The effect of actually attending remains unresolved. Assessment coverage of 97% in both groups is reassuring, though equal coverage alone does not rule out bias from missing outcomes.

Continued operation can therefore be justified on the committee’s stated grounds while further evaluation proceeds. A feasible next step is to finalize and begin the proposed randomized evaluation for students with unreliable internet, prespecifying outcomes, assigned-group analysis, and missing-data checks. Its results would concern the combined offer of tutoring, a hotspot, and flexible scheduling—not tutoring alone or the original offer. The original study leaves benefits for these students unresolved.
