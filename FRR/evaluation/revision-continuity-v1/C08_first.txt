The pilot supports a decline in recorded operational alerts, from 30/120 to 18/120 shipments, and in alert-related dispatch calls, from 43 to 27; independent dispatch logs confirm the latter. It does not establish that insulation reduced the prespecified temperature-excursion endpoint by 40%.

The firmware change made alerts require three consecutive minutes above 8°C instead of two, so alerts ceased to measure the research endpoint consistently across periods. The safety officer’s authorization permitted that operational change; it neither amended the endpoint nor established that two-minute excursions were harmless.

Applying the original endpoint to recoverable minute-level records yields 16/80 shipments before installation and 15/80 afterward. These results do not establish a substantial reduction, but they also do not establish no benefit: missing records disproportionately involve older devices, limiting representativeness, and the before-and-after comparison cannot isolate insulation from other changes.

The corrected memo appropriately withdraws the 40% exposure claim while preserving the operational findings. The dashboard’s “validated” benefit statement remains unsupported and should be corrected wherever it has informed subsequent summaries or decisions.

The possibility of greater benefit on hot routes remains unresolved because those routes were uncommon and lacked a suitable comparison. A feasible immediate analysis is to assess record availability by device age, route, and period, then calculate bounds showing how missing outcomes could alter the endpoint comparison. The drafted study using independent loggers and concurrent randomized comparisons remains the appropriate next collection step, with sufficient hot-weather coverage and the original endpoint fixed in advance. Operational alerts and dispatch workload should remain separately reported outcomes.
