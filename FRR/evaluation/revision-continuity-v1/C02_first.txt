The evaluation has not established that tutoring improves learning. The most credible current estimate is the effect of being offered access: 0.07 standard deviations on the prespecified, anonymously scored assessment, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22. This leaves both no benefit and a modest benefit plausible. Outcomes were available for 97% of each assigned group, although missing outcomes remain a limitation.

The original 0.31-standard-deviation estimate should no longer support efficacy claims or expansion decisions. It compared frequent attendees with everyone assigned ordinary support, excluding invited students who attended less often. Because prior grades and work schedules predicted attendance, that comparison did not preserve the protection of random assignment. The old planning slide should be corrected to reflect the withdrawal of its causal claim.

Several findings remain useful. Accurate logs show that 62% of invited students attended at least six sessions, and favorable participant interviews inform acceptability. These findings support feasibility and demand, but do not establish learning gains. Likewise, the teaching committee’s approval authorizes continued operation; it is not evidence of efficacy. The corrected estimate concerns offering access, not the causal effect of attending tutoring.

The effect for students with unreliable home internet remains unresolved because the original study included too few for a useful estimate. A feasible next step is to develop and preregister an adequately sized randomized trial of the proposed tutoring, hotspot, and flexible-scheduling package, with assessment follow-up and analysis retaining students in their assigned groups. Such a trial would estimate the effect of offering that combined package, not tutoring alone. No such study had begun when the corrected report was filed.
