The evaluation does not currently establish that tutoring improves learning. The strongest causal estimate is the effect of being offered access: 0.07 standard deviations on the prespecified assessment, with a 95% confidence interval from −0.08 to 0.22. This leaves both no benefit and a modest benefit plausible. Outcomes were available for 97% of each assigned group; the remaining missing outcomes warrant sensitivity checks.

The reported 0.31-standard-deviation advantage cannot support a causal claim. It compared frequent attendees with students assigned ordinary support, excluding invited students who attended less often. Because prior grades and work schedules predicted attendance, that comparison lost the protection of random assignment. The corrected analysis estimates the effect of an invitation, not the effect of attending tutoring.

Several findings remain useful: the attendance logs show that 62% of invited students attended at least six sessions, and favorable interviews provide evidence about participants’ experiences. Neither establishes learning gains. The teaching committee’s approval supports continued operation on feasibility and demand grounds; it does not supply evidence of efficacy. The planning slide claiming a proven 0.31-standard-deviation gain should be corrected, along with any materials repeating it.

The question about students with unreliable home internet remains unresolved because the original sample was too small for a useful estimate. A feasible next step is to develop and preregister the proposed trial combining tutoring access, a hotspot loan, and flexible scheduling, with adequate representation of those students and a prespecified learning outcome. Any resulting effect would concern that combined offer; attributing it specifically to tutoring would require additional comparisons. Continued operation can accompany this work, provided communications retain the distinction between service authorization and demonstrated learning benefit.
