The replication provides credible evidence that offering the combined tutoring, hotspot, and flexible-scheduling package improves assessment performance for students with unreliable home internet. Its preregistered analysis retained all 800 students in their assigned groups and estimated a 0.24-standard-deviation benefit (95% confidence interval, 0.10–0.38), with assessment outcomes available for 98% of both groups. This resolves an important earlier uncertainty for that population and package.

It does not vindicate the original 0.31-standard-deviation claim. That estimate compared frequent attendees with ordinary-support students and lost the protection of random assignment. The original corrected invitation estimate—0.07 standard deviations, with a confidence interval of −0.08 to 0.22—remains the appropriate result for the original offer. The two studies evaluated different offers and populations; their findings need not conflict.

Do not restore the old slide or use the replication to justify tutoring alone for every student. Replace the slide with an accurate account of the package’s benefit in the studied population. Because no group received tutoring without the hotspot and scheduling changes, the replication cannot isolate tutoring’s contribution or establish whether those supports are necessary. It also does not establish benefits for students with reliable internet.

Attendance findings remain usable: 62% reached six sessions under the original offer and 74% under the package. Favorable interviews remain evidence of acceptability, not learning gains. Committee approval permits operation; its announcement cannot change what the studies establish.

The evidence supports considering expansion of the tested package to comparable students, subject to delivery capacity and costs. Further randomized comparisons should examine tutoring alone versus the combined package and assess effects in broader student populations before universal expansion.
